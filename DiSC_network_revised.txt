Personality-Driven Attachment and Core Positioning in Social Networks: A DiSC-Based Simulation and Empirical Validation

Abstract. This study proposes a novel random network generation model grounded in the DiSC personality framework (Dominance, Influence, Steadiness, Compliance), in which each personality type follows a distinct attachment strategy: D-type nodes attach to high-betweenness nodes, I-type to high-degree nodes, S-type to highly cohesive hub nodes, and C-type to low-degree nodes. We developed a simulation platform and conducted experiments across 1,000 independently generated random networks (100–200 nodes, 200–1,200 edges). K-core decomposition revealed that the I group appeared most frequently in the network core at approximately 28.16% ± 3.24%, followed by the S group at 25.73% ± 3.45%, while the D and C groups accounted for about 25.60% ± 3.12% and 20.52% ± 2.89%, respectively. Validation across 7 real-world social networks (comprising 160 nodes in total) showed that, on average, 89.3% of core nodes belong to I and S groups (enriching them significantly relative to their 50% base-rate representation). These results suggest that Influence and Steadiness personality types are disproportionately positioned at the structural center of social networks. The software is available at link https://github.com/NamIT2k5/DiSC.NET.

Keywords: DiSC personality; social network analysis; centrality; random network generation; K-core decomposition

1. Introduction
Social network analysis has become a cornerstone methodology for understanding human relationships. Since Freeman’s formalization of centrality measures [1, 2] and Barabási and Albert’s (1999) preferential attachment model [3], researchers have sought to explain how individuals occupy structurally advantageous positions. Central nodes exert disproportionate influence on information flow and social cohesion [4, 5]. However, most network models treat nodes as homogeneous, neglecting the well-documented role of personality in shaping social connections. The DiSC framework, originating from Marston’s (1928) theory [6], classifies individuals along four behavioral dimensions: Dominance (assertive, results-oriented), Influence (enthusiastic, sociable), Steadiness (cooperative, loyal), and Compliance (analytical, cautious) [7, 8]. With over 50 million assessments administered worldwide [9], DiSC is among the most widely used behavioral frameworks, yet it has not been integrated into computational network models.

Several research strands inform the present study. In personality–network research, Fang et al. (2015) meta-analyzed 138 samples and found that extraversion and self-monitoring predicted indegree centrality and brokerage [10]. Morelli et al. (2017) showed that empathy and well-being differentially predicted centrality in trust-based versus fun-based networks [11]. Clifton et al. (2009) demonstrated that narcissistic traits correlate positively with betweenness centrality, while avoidant traits correlate negatively [12]. In network generation, the BA model [3] introduced preferential attachment producing scale-free networks, extended by the Bianconi–Barabási fitness model [13] and the Watts–Strogatz small-world model [14]. K-core decomposition, proposed by Seidman (1983) [15], identifies maximal subgraphs where every node has at least k connections, and Kitsak et al. (2010) showed K-core position outperforms degree in predicting spreading influence [16]. Despite this extensive literature, no prior study has integrated personality-driven attachment strategies into a generative network model using an established behavioral framework.

To address this gap, we make four contributions: (1) We propose a DiSC-based random network model where four personality types follow distinct attachment strategies based on different centrality measures. (2) We develop a simulation platform for configurable network generation. (3) We validate through 1,000 randomly generated networks, analyzing K-core structure. (4) We cross-validate on 7 real-world social networks, where I and S types together account for an average of 89.3% of core nodes.

2. Methods and Materials

2.1. Algorithm
The algorithm initialises a small and connected seed graph of m0 nodes. At each step, a new node v is created with a randomly assigned DiSC type. The node forms connections to existing nodes according to its type-specific probability function. Centrality measures are recalculated at every step. The process continues until the target number of nodes N = [100, 200] and edges E = [200, 1200] are reached. The generator is controlled by four parameters: the seed size m0 (a small and connected graph, e.g., a clique), the number of edges m added by each new node, the DiSC by probability pi = (0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25), and the targets N and E. The values of m0 and m are configured per run so that the final graph lies within the target ranges. The complete procedure is given in Algorithm 1, where f_T(u) is the type-specific attachment weight defined in Section 3.1.

Algorithm 1. DiSC-based network generation.
Input : N, E (targets); m0 (seed size); m (edges per new node);
        DiSC probability pi = (0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25)
Output: graph G = (V, Eg) with a DiSC label on every node
1: G <- connected seed graph with m0 nodes
2: while |V| < N or |Eg| < E do
3:     create node v; T <- sample DiSC type from pi
4:     compute degree(u), betweenness(u), clustering(u) for all u in V
5:     for all u in V: w(u) <- f_T(u) // type weight (Sec. 3.1)
6:     P(v,u) <- w(u) / sum_{x in V} w(x)
7:     pick m distinct targets, prob. proportional to P(v,u)
8:     add v and the chosen edges (v,u) to G
9: end while
10: return G

Because betweenness centrality is recomputed as the network grows, its repeated all-pairs shortest-path evaluation dominates the running time, giving an overall complexity on the order of O(N·|V|·|Eg|) for the betweenness-dependent (D-type) updates; degree- and clustering-based weights are obtained incrementally. A unit additive smoothing (degree(u)+1) is used in the C-type weight so that every probability remains well defined for low- or zero-degree targets.

2.1.1. Justification of Parameter Choices and Systematic Sensitivity Analysis
We emphasize that the coefficients and exponents in Eqs. (1)-(4) are theoretically motivated rather than empirically fitted. The I-type cubic exponent (3) amplifies preferential attachment beyond the classical linear Barabási-Albert rule to instantiate strongly hub-seeking behavior consistent with the extraversion-centrality link reported by Fang et al. [10]; the D-type blend of betweenness and degree (0.6/0.4) reflects the joint boundary-spanning and assertive-popularity profile documented by Clifton et al. [12] and Burt et al. [17]; the S-type joint degree x clustering exponent (2.5) operationalizes a "hub embedded in a cohesive cluster" mechanism consistent with the closeness/embeddedness findings of Morelli et al. [11]; and the C-type inverse-square exponent instantiates strong periphery-avoidance consistent with the cautious, risk-averse Compliance profile [8]. Because these values were set a priori to produce qualitatively distinct, theoretically interpretable attachment regimes rather than estimated from behavioral data, the model should be read as a mechanistic hypothesis-generation tool: the reported structural differences demonstrate what the assumed mechanisms imply, not that real DiSC types follow them.

To determine whether the reported K-core dominance of I and S groups is robust or merely an artifact of the specific coefficients chosen, we conducted a systematic parameter sensitivity analysis: each exponent (1.5, 3, 2.5, -2) and blending weight (0.6/0.4) was perturbed one at a time by ±20%. For every perturbed configuration, a 100-network simulation was run, and the resulting ranking of D, I, S, and C core proportions was checked for stability. Table 2.1 shows that the overall ranking (I > S ≈ D > C) is robust against these perturbations.

2.2. Experimental Design
We generated 1,000 independent random networks and analyzed the personality distribution within K-core structures. For real-world validation, we applied K-core decomposition to 7 empirical classroom and community social networks consisting of a total of N_real = 160 members. Each member completed a standardized DiSC assessment, establishing the base-rate distribution of personality types across the full membership as approximately balanced (25.0% D, 25.0% I, 25.0% S, 25.0% C).

Throughout this paper, the innermost core of a network denotes its maximum k-core, that is, the k-core with the largest k for which a non-empty subgraph exists; a node is counted as a core member if it belongs to that subgraph. Across the 7 networks, K-core decomposition was applied; the resulting innermost cores (with the maximum coreness k_max ranging from 4 to 8) contained a total of c = 38 core nodes across all networks, whose DiSC composition is reported in Section 3.4. We report both the base-rate distribution of personality types in the full membership and their core enrichment rates to establish statistical validity.

3. Results

3.1. DiSC-based Network Generation Model
We propose a model in which new nodes are assigned a DiSC personality type with equal prior probability (25% per type). The percentages reported in this paper (28.16% I, 25.73% S, 25.60% D, 20.52% C) denote the resulting K-core composition, not the assignment probabilities. Each type follows a distinct attachment strategy reflecting observed social bonding tendencies: D-type nodes preferentially attach to high-betweenness nodes (strategic control-seeking), I-type to high-degree nodes (hub-seeking), S-type nodes to highly cohesive hub nodes (stability-seeking behavior), and C-type to low-degree nodes (periphery-seeking). The attachment probabilities are:

P(v→u | D) = (\alpha_D * Betweenness(u) + (1-\alpha_D)*Degree(u))^{\beta_D} / \sum_{x\in V} (\alpha_D * Betweenness(x) + (1-\alpha_D)*Degree(x))^{\beta_D}    (1)

P(v→u | I) = Degree(u)^{\beta_I} / \sum_{x\in V} Degree(x)^{\beta_I}    (2)

P(v→u | S) = (Clustering(u) * Degree(u))^{\beta_S} / \sum_{x\in V} (Clustering(x) * Degree(x))^{\beta_S}    (3)

P(v→u | C) = (Degree(u)+1)^{-\beta_C} / \sum_{x\in V} (Degree(x)+1)^{-\beta_C}    (4)

Explicitly, the unnormalized attachment weight wT(u) of a candidate target u under each type is:
* wD(u) = (0.6 * Betweenness(u) + 0.4 * Degree(u))^{1.5}
* wI(u) = Degree(u)^{3.0}
* wS(u) = (Clustering(u) * Degree(u))^{2.5}
* wC(u) = (Degree(u) + 1)^{-2.0}

The target of each new edge is then drawn with probability P(v→u | T) = wT(u) / Σ_(x∈V) wT(x). The +1 term in wC keeps the weight finite for isolated (degree-0) nodes; D, I and S follow preferential (centrality-seeking) attachment, whereas C follows anti-preferential (periphery-seeking) attachment in Fig. 1.

3.2. Simulation Software
The simulation software was implemented with a modular architecture capable of batch-generating networks under configurable parameters. The system accepts input parameters including the number of networks (NetworkNumber), the node range (100–200), and the edge range (200–1,200). For each network instance, the generator initializes a seed graph, iteratively adds nodes with DiSC-assigned attachment strategies, recalculates centrality measures at each step, and outputs the completed network with full annotation. The software architecture in Fig. 2 comprises four principal modules: (1) Network Generator—creates nodes, assigns DiSC types, implements attachment; (2) Centrality Calculator—computes betweenness, degree, clustering at each iteration; (3) K-core Analyzer—performs decomposition, extracts core membership; (4) Statistics Aggregator—compiles results across network instances. Input: NetworkNumber, node range [100, 200], edge range [200, 1200]. Output: annotated random networks with personality-labeled K-core structures.

3.3. K-core Analysis of Simulated Networks
K-core decomposition across 1,000 networks revealed the average personality distribution in the innermost core in Fig. 3: S (Steadiness) = 25.73%, I (Influence) = 28.16%, C (Compliance) = 20.52%, D (Dominance) = 25.60%. point estimates show that Influence (I) and Steadiness (S) groups consistently occupy the largest shares of the innermost core.

To ensure statistical rigor, we computed the run-to-run standard deviation and constructed 95% confidence intervals (using bootstrapping with 10,000 resamples over the 1,000 simulated networks) for the mean core proportions of each type, reported in Table 3.1.

One-way ANOVA conducted on the core proportions across the 1,000 runs shows a highly significant difference in core representation between the four groups (F(3, 3996) = 1247.3, p < 0.001). Tukey's HSD post-hoc test confirms that all pairwise differences are statistically significant (p < 0.01) except for the S-D pair (p = 0.73).

4. Discussions
This study establishes a formal, mechanistic link between DiSC personality dimensions and network formation. Unlike prior correlational studies [10-12], our generative model illustrates how personality-specific attachment strategies can, by construction, reproduce the observed structural outcomes; because these strategies are specified a priori, the simulation should be read as a mechanistic hypothesis rather than as confirmation that real DiSC types follow them. As shown in the sensitivity analysis (Section 2.1.1), the observed core dominance of I and S groups is a robust property of the model under minor parameter perturbations, confirming the stability of the hypothesized mechanism.

The I-type’s hub-seeking strategy mirrors the classical Barabási–Albert preferential attachment [3], embedding I-type nodes in densely connected neighborhoods. The S-type’s closeness-seeking strategy achieves centrality through a complementary mechanism: by connecting to nodes closest to all others, S-type nodes gain structural proximity rather than direct popularity. The synergy of these two strategies—popularity-based and proximity-based—creates the dominance of I and S groups in the network core observed both in simulation (53.89% innermost core share) and real-world data (89.3% core share).

The model also explains why D and C types tend toward the periphery. D-type nodes target high-betweenness positions, which lie between dense clusters rather than within them, positioning D-types as boundary spanners and gatekeepers rather than core members—consistent with the organizational behavior literature [17, 18]. C-type nodes explicitly avoid dense hubs by targeting low-degree nodes, reflecting the cautious, risk-averse behavioral profile of the Compliance dimension [8]. The real-world validation shows a much stronger version of the simulation trend: while the simulated I+S share is only about 54%, the empirical networks reveal I and S at 89.3% of core nodes. This large gap warrants caution, as it may partly reflect base-rate or sampling differences in the validation networks. One possible contributing factor is cumulative network dynamics—as I and S nodes progressively attract further connections through their core positions, a rich-get-richer effect concentrates these types at the structural center over time.

Our approach extends the Barabási–Albert framework by introducing heterogeneous, psychologically motivated attachment strategies. While the BA model assumes all nodes follow identical degree-proportional attachment, and the Bianconi–Barabási fitness model uses abstract parameters [13], our model grounds heterogeneity in an established behavioral framework with over 50 million real-world assessments [9]. The use of K-core decomposition is particularly appropriate as it captures recursive core membership: a node belongs to the K-core because its neighbors also have sufficient connections [15, 16], making it a robust indicator of deep structural embeddedness beyond simple degree or betweenness measures.

The practical implications are substantial. Organizations seeking to maximize network connectivity should strategically position I and S types, who naturally build the connective tissue of social networks. For influence maximization in viral marketing or public health campaigns, targeting I and S types for seeding would yield greater structural diffusion since these types populate the densest network regions [16]. In community detection, incorporating DiSC personality metadata as node attributes could improve identification accuracy in networks where structural data alone is insufficient [19, 20].

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The equal probability assignment of DiSC types (25% each) is simplifying; real populations have non-uniform distributions. The model assumes fixed, single DiSC types, whereas individuals may exhibit blended or context-dependent profiles. Although the empirical validation was extended to 7 social networks (160 nodes in total), larger organizational networks should be tested to confirm the generality of these findings. Finally, the coefficients underlying the attachment model were set from theory rather than fitted to data, meaning the model serves as an explanatory framework rather than a calibrated predictive model. The model does not currently account for edge dissolution or rewiring dynamics. Future work should investigate sensitivity to non-uniform personality distributions, incorporate temporal dynamics, and validate across online, organizational, and educational networks.

As a speculative interpretation that lies beyond the empirical scope of this study: from a social network science perspective, collective decisions formed through decentralized mechanisms such as majority voting tend to amplify the influence of structurally central actors, particularly those in the I and S groups. These agents typically occupy high-centrality positions (e.g., degree and closeness centrality), allowing them to act as effective hubs in opinion diffusion and thereby emerge as natural drivers of collective behavior. However, structural prominence does not necessarily translate into optimal long-term outcomes for the system. Empirical evidence from the Universe 25 experiment conducted by John B. Calhoun (1973) [21] shows that even in resource-abundant environments, social systems may collapse due to the erosion of functional role differentiation. By analogy, a materially abundant human society may marginalize functionally critical groups such as D (execution-oriented) and C (analytical and science-oriented), while over-amplifying socially central like I and S but less strategically oriented actors. Therefore, leadership selection should not rely solely on emergent network influence but must be guided by criteria aligned with the system’s long-term developmental objectives.

In conclusion, this study proposed and validated a DiSC-based random network generation model integrating personality-driven attachment strategies. Through simulation across 1,000 networks and real-world cross-validation, we demonstrated that Influence and Steadiness personality types consistently dominate the core structure of social networks. These findings advance both network science and applied personality psychology, providing actionable insights for organizational design, influence maximization, and community detection, and opening new avenues for personality-aware network modeling.
